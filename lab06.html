Lab 6: Interactive Hometown Map

Lake Stevens and Seattle, Washington

DCDA 40833 Skills Portfolio | Spring 2026

Lab Overview

For Lab 6, I created an interactive map focused on Lake Stevens and Seattle, Washington. I chose these areas because they gave me a mix of smaller hometown-style places and bigger Seattle landmarks that would be useful for someone visiting the area.

The map includes public locations like parks, trails, cultural places, landmarks, and sports locations. I did not include any private home addresses because this map is meant to be public-facing.

This lab brought together three parts: a custom Mapbox basemap, a CSV file of hometown locations, and a Python-generated Folium map with markers and pop-ups.

Design Inspiration

My design inspiration came from the Pacific Northwest and the Lake Stevens and Seattle area. I wanted the map to feel calm, outdoorsy, and easy to read. Since the area has lakes, water, trees, trails, and city landmarks, I wanted the basemap to have a softer look instead of feeling too harsh or crowded.

I used a simple custom Mapbox style so the markers would stand out. My goal was not to make the basemap overpower the locations. I wanted it to support the story of the map and help visitors focus on the places I selected.

Hometown Locations Dataset

For my CSV file, I selected public places that would give a visitor a good feel for Lake Stevens and Seattle. I wanted a mix of outdoor places, community locations, cultural stops, landmarks, and sports-related places.

The dataset included the location name, address, type, description, and image URL. Python used the addresses from the CSV file to geocode each location and place the markers on the map.

Types of Locations Included

  • Parks and waterfront areas
  • Community and cultural locations
  • Trails and recreation spots
  • Seattle landmarks
  • Sports and visitor destinations

Code Understanding

The Python script read my CSV file, sent each address to Mapbox for geocoding, and returned latitude and longitude coordinates. Then it created a Folium map centered around the Lake Stevens and Seattle area.

After that, the script added markers to the map. Each marker used a different color based on the location type, such as park, recreation, cultural, landmark, community, or sports. The pop-ups included the location name, type, description, and image.

I understand the overall logic of the code, but I would still need help writing all of it from scratch. I understand what each section is supposed to do, and I can make small edits like changing file names, marker colors, map center, zoom level, and pop-up text.

Reflection

Design Inspiration

My design inspiration came from the feeling of the Pacific Northwest. I wanted the map to feel connected to water, trees, trails, and a calm outdoor style. Since I used Lake Stevens and Seattle, I wanted the map to feel like something a visitor could actually use, but still have a personal and creative look. I kept the colors softer so the basemap would not compete with the markers.

Cartographic Challenges

The biggest cartographic challenge was thinking about scale. A map can look fine when zoomed in, but then look too empty or too crowded when zoomed out. I had to think about what information should be visible and what might be distracting. Since my markers were the most important part, I wanted the basemap to stay simple enough that the locations were easy to see.

I also had to think about balance. I wanted the map to look designed, but it still needed to function like a real map. If the colors were too strong or the map had too many details, the viewer might pay more attention to the background than the locations.

Technical Challenges

The technical part was the hardest because several pieces had to work together. I needed the CSV file, the Mapbox token, the Mapbox style ID, the Python script, the geocoding, and the final HTML map to all connect correctly. I had trouble with the basemap at first because the markers showed up, but the background did not look right. That helped me understand that the marker data and the basemap are separate pieces.

I used AI help to troubleshoot the code, especially when I was confused about the Mapbox style ID, the tile URL, and why the map was showing markers but not the full background. I learned that small details matter a lot. One wrong file name, style ID, folder path, or token can keep the map from working correctly.

Conclusion

This lab helped me see how mapping combines design, data, and coding. It was not just about making a map look nice. I had to choose meaningful locations, organize the data, create a working map, and think about how the design affected the viewer. My biggest takeaway is that interactive maps need both personality and clarity. The map should feel personal, but it still has to be easy to use.
